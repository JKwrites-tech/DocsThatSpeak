# The Doc You Didn't Write Is the Ticket You'll Get

Not every support ticket points back to documentation. Some are product bugs. Some are UX problems. Some are just edge cases that nobody anticipated.

But a surprising number of them are things your docs could have prevented. And once you start reading your ticket queue that way, it becomes one of the most useful signals you have.

---

## Why documentation gaps are hard to spot

Nobody files a ticket that says *"I need a troubleshooting guide for the invoice upload failure at step 3."* They say *"it's broken"* or *"I don't get it"* or they say nothing at all and quietly stop using the feature.

That is what makes documentation gaps easy to miss. They do not announce themselves. They show up as frustrated users, repeated questions, and support load that just keeps growing.

By the time a pattern becomes obvious enough to notice, a lot of users have already had a bad experience. The earlier you start reading the ticket queue as a content signal, the faster you get ahead of it.

The goal here is not to document your way out of every support ticket. It is to find the ones where good documentation would have made a real difference. Hover each step to see how.

## One metric worth tracking: ticket deflection rate

When you improve documentation and fewer users need to contact support, that is called ticket deflection. It is one of the clearest ways to show the business value of your documentation work, because it translates directly into time and cost saved.

## What this looks like when it is working well

A documentation approach built on support signals tends to have three things in common.

**Reactive coverage:** There is a dedicated article for every topic that consistently drives support tickets. Not a general overview that mentions the topic in passing. A focused piece that answers the exact question users are asking.

**Proactive placement:** The article is not just sitting in the help portal. It is surfaced inside the product at the moment a user is likely to need it. For example, as a contextual help link next to a setting, or as a tooltip on a step that commonly causes confusion.

**A process for keeping it current:** Support agents flag documentation-related tickets, writers review those flags regularly, and articles are updated in the same cycle as the product changes that made them outdated.

---

## Try this this week

Pull your last 30 support tickets. For each one, ask: if a user had searched our docs before raising this ticket, would they have found a useful answer?

You do not need a formal audit to answer that. A quick pass through 30 tickets will show you more about where your documentation needs work than most content reviews ever will.
